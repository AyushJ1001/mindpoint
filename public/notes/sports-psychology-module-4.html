Module 4 · Team dynamics and motivation

.1 Task cohesion and social cohesion

Task cohesion concerns commitment to shared objectives and coordinated work. Social cohesion concerns relationships and belonging. A friendly team can be poorly coordinated; a technically coordinated team can contain relationship difficulties. Both dimensions deserve attention, but neither guarantees a result.

Clarify roles, decision processes and expectations. Ambiguity can create conflict even when everyone cares about the team. A substitute may need to know how selection decisions are communicated. A captain may need clarity about what can be decided independently.

Psychological support should not be used to make unclear organisational arrangements seem acceptable.

4.2 Communication that can be checked

Effective communication includes a message, an opportunity to clarify and confirmation of shared understanding. Instructions can fail because they are too complex, delivered at the wrong time or inaccessible in language or format. A nod does not always mean understanding.

A practical technique is a brief check-back: “What is the agreed role for this drill?” This is not a test designed to embarrass the athlete. It checks the explanation. In a multilingual team, ask which language or demonstration format helps rather than assuming everyone can use one dominant language equally well.

Dialogue · Correcting a misunderstanding

Coach: “You ignored the plan.” Player: “I thought I was covering the other side.” Revised response: “We had different understandings of the role. Let us check what each of us heard and agree the next drill.” This response does not remove accountability. It establishes whether the problem was a decision, an instruction or a shared misunderstanding before choosing a correction.

4.3 Leadership and power

Transformational, democratic and autocratic leadership describe different ways of directing and supporting a team. Treat them as broad descriptions of behaviour, not fixed personality types or a ranking of good and bad people. Consultation can support participation; clear direction can be important in a time- critical task. Neither justifies disrespect or exclusion.

Leadership also involves how feedback, mistakes and concerns are handled. Do players know where to report a problem? Can they disagree without retaliation? Are selection decisions explained appropriately? Does the leader acknowledge uncertainty? These questions reveal the working environment more clearly than a charismatic speech.

4.4 Motivation in a team context

Rewards, recognition, learning opportunities and responsibility can influence motivation. Their meaning depends on the athlete and the setting. Public praise may feel encouraging to one player and embarrassing to another. A reward system that celebrates only outcomes may discourage reporting injury or asking for help.

Ask whether the motivational strategy supports the agreed goal without creating avoidable pressure. A useful review examines participation, learning and wellbeing. “The players worked harder” is incomplete when the same period involved exhaustion or unsafe behaviour.

4.5 Conflict, diversity and team agreements

Conflict can arise from roles, resources, communication and incompatible expectations. Begin by describing the issue and hearing relevant perspectives. Do not assume that shared cultural identity means shared preferences, or that a rural, urban, linguistic or regional background predicts a person's social skills.

A team agreement can specify respectful feedback, privacy, help-seeking, language access and how concerns are escalated. It should not require disclosure of mental health information to teammates. Clinical information-sharing needs a separate, clear and appropriate basis.

Module consolidation: Compare task and social cohesion. Identify a communication adjustment that improves access without attributing the difficulty to a player's character.

4.6 Roles, shared expectations and leadership

A team is more than a collection of individually skilled athletes. Members must coordinate actions under changing conditions, often with limited time to explain themselves. Role clarity concerns understanding what is expected, when a responsibility changes and how to communicate uncertainty. It is possible for everyone to care strongly about a team and still work at cross-purposes because these expectations are unclear.

Formal roles include captain, starter, substitute or designated communicator. Informal roles may include welcoming new members, calming conflict or explaining instructions. Informal contributions can be valuable, but they should not become unrecognised obligations imposed on one player. A teammate who is trusted by others is not automatically responsible for managing their mental health. Leadership includes knowing when a concern belongs with a qualified professional or organisational process.

In a fictional football team, a substitute is told to “bring energy” but receives no tactical explanation. After entering the game, she presses forward while teammates expect her to cover a space. The resulting criticism concerns an expectation that was never made explicit. A useful review asks what was communicated before entry, how understanding was checked and which cue would signal a change. It does not begin with a diagnosis of selfishness.

Shared expectations should be concrete enough to check. Instead of “communicate better,” a team might agree who calls a defensive change and how others acknowledge it. Instead of

“respect everyone,” the agreement can specify that feedback addresses an action, not a person's worth. These arrangements are revisited when personnel, language, experience or competition conditions change.

Effective leadership may involve consultation, explanation or rapid direction depending on the situation. The method should fit the task without excusing humiliation or retaliation. An athlete's disagreement can reveal missing information rather than disloyalty. A constructive review distinguishes unclear instruction, a mistaken decision, a skill limitation and deliberate disregard. Each calls for a different response. Clear roles make accountability fairer because people can understand what was expected and how their actions affected the shared task.

4.7 Working through team conflict

Conflict can concern an observable event, an interpretation of that event and the impact on the relationship. These elements are often compressed into a label: “She does not care about the team.” Slowing the account makes a useful conversation possible. What happened? What meaning did each person give it? What practical or emotional consequence followed? What agreement is needed for future work?

In a fictional relay team, Maya changes the agreed practice time after receiving a timetable update. She posts the message in a group chat. Ritu does not see it and arrives late. Maya interprets the lateness as indifference; Ritu experiences the public criticism as unfair. The first issue is not which person has a worse attitude. It is how the change was communicated and whether receipt was confirmed.

A constructive conversation begins with a specific description. Maya might say, “We started without you after the time changed.” Ritu can explain that she did not receive the update. The discussion then separates impact from intention: the team lost practice time, but the available information does not establish deliberate disregard. An agreed check-back for late changes may address the immediate problem more directly than demanding an apology for an assumed motive.

This does not mean every conflict is an innocent misunderstanding. Repeated exclusion, discriminatory remarks, threats or retaliation require appropriate organisational and safeguarding responses. A peer conversation is not a substitute for those processes. Nor should a person be required to meet someone who makes them unsafe in order to show team spirit. Power differences influence what can be said freely.

After an ordinary disagreement, review the agreement in practice. Did the new communication method work? Were responsibilities shared fairly? Can members raise a concern without public embarrassment? If the same problem repeats, examine whether the arrangement is realistic rather than simply asking for more goodwill. Conflict resolution is not the disappearance of all negative feeling. It is a clearer, safer and more workable way to coordinate behaviour while recognising that relationships may take longer to repair.
