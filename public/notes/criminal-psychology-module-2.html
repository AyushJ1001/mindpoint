Module 2 · Criminal profiling and interviewing techniques

.1 What a profile can and cannot do

Offender profiling attempts to develop hypotheses about an unknown offender or links among incidents from available information. Such hypotheses may suggest questions or investigative leads. They do not establish identity, guilt, a diagnosis or a person's private motives. The quality of any inference depends on the underlying evidence and the method used.

An apparently coherent profile can feel convincing because its details form a story. This does not make it accurate. Some descriptions are broad enough to fit many people. Others may reflect stereotypes or information already known to the analyst. A responsible account identifies uncertainty, alternatives and how a hypothesis could be challenged.

The value of a profile depends on its reasoning and evidence, not on a dramatic origin story.

Ask which observations support it, what remains uncertain and whether it produces information that can be checked independently.

2.2 Organising information without inventing a biography

Separate scene observations, witness reports, physical evidence, contextual information and interpretations. Record where information came from and whether it has been independently checked. A detail repeated by several people may still originate from one source.

Terms such as modus operandi and signature are sometimes used in discussions of patterns.

Modus operandi concerns methods associated with an act. A proposed signature concerns features interpreted as having additional psychological meaning. These distinctions should be treated cautiously: an unusual feature does not reveal a complete personality, and similar acts do not automatically have the same perpetrator.

Victimology should examine relevant circumstances respectfully. It must not imply that a victim caused the offence or deserved harm. Avoid language that treats some people as disposable or inherently suitable targets. The purpose of analysis does not remove the need for dignity.

2.3 Hypothesis testing and confirmation bias

Confirmation bias can involve preferentially noticing information that supports an existing explanation. A practical safeguard is to state competing hypotheses and identify evidence that would distinguish them. This does not require treating every possibility as equally likely; it requires keeping the reasoning open to correction.

Case study · Similar thefts in two offices

Two fictional offices report missing equipment after an evening event. One student concludes that the same organised offender acted in both places. Alternatives include separate incidents, a recording error or authorised movement of equipment. The next step is to examine records and independently gathered information, not to infer a suspect's age, background or personality. Similarity is a reason to investigate, not proof of a link.

A useful case note has four headings: information, source, interpretation and uncertainty. It also asks what would change the current view. A profile that cannot be revised when new evidence appears is not functioning as a testable hypothesis.

2.4 Interviewing aims and professional roles

An investigative interview seeks accurate and relevant information within legal and ethical safeguards. It is not successful merely because a person confesses, agrees or becomes emotional. Information quality and the conditions under which it was obtained matter. The

Méndez Principles emphasise science, law, ethics, safeguards, vulnerability, training and institutional accountability.

A therapeutic conversation and an investigative interview have different purposes and information-sharing arrangements. A person should not be misled into believing that an evidence-gathering interaction is confidential therapy. Qualified professionals need clarity about role conflicts and who will use their information.

An introductory classroom can practise distinguishing open and leading questions using a neutral fictional event. It should not imitate coercive interrogation, stage false accusations or pressure participants to confess to something.

2.5 Coercive approaches: critical understanding, not a practice toolkit

Confrontation, good-cop/bad-cop, minimisation, maximisation, fabricated evidence and pressure through silence raise serious questions about reliability and fairness. A statement produced under pressure may reflect compliance, confusion or a wish to end the interaction rather than reliable knowledge.

A promise of leniency, a threat or misleading information can affect what a person says. The fact that a statement contains details does not alone establish reliability; details can be supplied or reinforced during questioning. Independent corroboration and a record of how information emerged remain important.

“Rapport” should not mean disguising coercion with a friendly tone. Respectful engagement includes honest role explanations and appropriate safeguards. A person in a vulnerable situation may require legal support, an interpreter, communication adjustments or medical assessment. These needs should be addressed rather than exploited.

2.6 The PEACE framework

PEACE is a structured, non-coercive interviewing framework. The letters commonly refer to

Planning and Preparation; Engage and Explain; Account, Clarification and Challenge; Closure; and Evaluation. This is an introduction to the framework, not training to conduct formal interviews.

Planning concerns the purpose, information available, questions and safeguards. Engage and

Explain concerns role, process and understanding. Account involves allowing the person to describe relevant events before focused clarification. Challenge should mean addressing inconsistencies fairly with evidence, not intimidation. Closure checks the account and explains next steps. Evaluation examines information and the interviewing process; it is not reading guilt from body language.

Introductory learning question

Planning and Preparation

What is the purpose, and what is known independently?

2.7 Question wording and memory

An open invitation allows the person to describe what they remember. A leading question supplies or suggests information. “What did you notice near the entrance?” differs from “Was the man in the red jacket waiting by the entrance?” The second introduces a person, clothing and location that may not have been reported.

Cognitive interviewing aims to support recall through specialised methods, not to encourage invention. Memory-support procedures require training and adaptation. An introductory learner should not conduct trauma-memory recovery, ask someone to imagine unobserved events or treat confidence as proof of accuracy.

A useful classroom exercise compares two sets of questions about a neutral fictional meeting.

Mark which words introduce assumptions and rewrite them. Do not deliberately create distress or attempt to implant a false memory in another participant.

2.8 Demeanour and deception claims

Nervousness, reduced eye contact, unusual speech or a change in posture can have many explanations. They do not establish deception. A calm manner does not establish truthfulness.

Language, culture, disability, fatigue, fear and the setting can affect behaviour. Body language and voice stress should not be treated as diagnostic tests of deception.

The reasoning is straightforward: when an observation has several plausible causes, it cannot by itself identify one cause. A mismatch between two accounts may justify clarification, but it does not prove which account is accurate or why they differ. Evaluate content, conditions and independent evidence rather than relying on a behavioural stereotype.

2.9 Custodial rights and the Indian legal framework

For Indian matters, the relevant legal professional must check the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita,

Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, along with constitutional safeguards, commencement and transitional provisions, applicable amendments and case law.

For one concrete example, section 38 of the BNSS provides for an arrested person to meet an advocate of their choice during interrogation, though not throughout it. This should not be rewritten as a general discretionary permission that can simply be removed because consultation is inconvenient. Its application belongs with qualified legal advice.

Legal rights and procedures need advice from an appropriately qualified legal professional.

Verify the applicable law and professional role before making a legal claim. A psychologist should not promise a legal outcome or treat a clinical opinion as a judicial decision.

2.10 Technology, databases and forensic evidence

Mapping, databases, digital records, facial recognition, voice analysis and AI have distinct functions and limitations. These technologies have different purposes. Matching a record, detecting a pattern and establishing responsibility are different steps. A database result may generate a lead without proving that a person committed an offence.

Ask about data quality, access, error rates, validation, bias and the legal basis for collection and use. A system trained on historical records may reflect how those records were produced. A map of recorded incidents is not a direct map of individual criminality. Human review does not automatically remove error unless the review itself is structured and accountable.

Claims about a particular deployment, database or successful use require supporting documentation. For real-world use, consult the responsible agency's documentation and the applicable law. Students should not collect classmates' biometric or sensitive data to imitate an investigative system.

2.11 A worked information-quality exercise

A fictional report says: “The interviewee looked away, so the account was false. The profile matched their personality. They eventually agreed with the interviewer.” None of these statements establishes guilt. Looking away has multiple explanations. A personality match may be broad or speculative. Agreement may be influenced by the questioning conditions.

A stronger summary is: “The interviewee's account differs from the available record on one time point. The source of that record and the interview conditions need review. No conclusion about guilt is made here.” This wording does not ignore inconsistency; it places it at the correct level of inference.

Module consolidation: Distinguish a lead, a hypothesis, corroborated information and a legal finding. Explain why a confession is not a substitute for evaluating evidence and the conditions of questioning.

MODULE 02

2.12 Building an evidence timeline

An evidence timeline organises information about an event without assuming that every item is equally reliable. It distinguishes what was directly observed, what was reported later, what appears in a record and what has been inferred. The source and timing of each piece of information matter. A detail repeated by several people may still originate from one account.

Start with a defined question and record the information available. Identify uncertainty about times, locations and sequence. A timestamp may be accurate, inaccurate or refer to a different action than assumed. A witness may remember an event well but be uncertain about its exact time. The task is to examine these differences rather than force them into a predetermined story.

In a fictional office case, a witness says a person left at six, while an access record shows a card used at six fifteen. Several explanations are possible: the witness's estimate may be approximate, the record may reflect another entrance or the card may have been used under circumstances not yet understood. The discrepancy matters, but it does not by itself prove dishonesty or identity.

A useful analysis separates an observation from its interpretation. “A card registered at the entrance” is different from “the cardholder committed the theft.” Independent corroboration may strengthen or weaken an account. Information gathered after a leading interview should also be considered in light of how it emerged. Confidence, repetition and narrative detail are not substitutes for examining the source.

The timeline should remain open to correction. New evidence may change an earlier assumption or show that a proposed link is unsupported. Record the change rather than preserving the original theory because time has already been invested in it. Introductory learners can practise this reasoning with neutral fictional material. The aim is not to conduct an investigation, but to understand why careful organisation, source evaluation and explicit uncertainty are essential before moving from information to a conclusion about what occurred or who was responsible.

MODULE 02

2.13 Question wording and an information-gathering conversation

Questions influence what information is offered and how it is framed. An open invitation allows the person to describe relevant events in their own words. A focused question clarifies a point already raised. A leading question suggests information or a preferred answer. The distinction matters because an apparently detailed account may contain elements introduced by the interviewer.

In a neutral fictional example, a witness describes a meeting in a reception area. “Tell me what you remember about arriving” is an open invitation. “You mentioned a bag; where did you notice it?” follows reported information. “Was the suspicious man carrying a black bag?” introduces a person, a judgement and a colour that may not have appeared in the account.

These added assumptions can influence the response.

A constructive exchange might proceed as follows. Interviewer: “What happened after you entered?” Witness: “I waited near the desk.” Interviewer: “You said you waited near the desk.

What could you see from there?” Witness: “Mostly the entrance; I could not see the corridor.”

The clarification establishes a limit to observation instead of encouraging the witness to fill an unseen part of the event.

It is also important to distinguish not remembering from refusing to answer and from not understanding. Repeating a question insistently may imply that the first answer was unacceptable. A person may then guess or change wording to satisfy the interviewer.

Appropriate explanation, communication support and safeguards help protect the quality of information.

Formal investigative interviewing requires specialised training and the relevant legal framework. A classroom exercise can examine wording without simulating coercion or trying to implant a memory. The learning goal is to recognise where a question supplies an answer, assumes guilt or overlooks a communication need. Good information gathering values accurate limits as well as details. “I did not see that part” can be important evidence, and it should not be treated as a failure that the interviewer must overcome through pressure.

MODULE 02

2.14 Memory, confidence and corroboration

Memory is not a complete recording that can be replayed without influence. Attention during an event, the passage of time, later information and retrieval conditions can affect what is remembered. This does not mean that memory is generally useless or that a particular account is false. It means that reliability should be evaluated through the conditions and evidence rather than assumed from vividness alone.

Confidence and accuracy are related in ways that depend on the task and circumstances. A confident statement is not automatically correct, and uncertainty is not automatically evidence of dishonesty. Feedback, repetition and information received after an event can change confidence. It is therefore useful to consider what the person initially reported and how later accounts developed.

In a fictional case, two colleagues discuss an incident before speaking separately to an interviewer. Their accounts later contain similar details. The similarity may reflect shared observation, conversation between them or both. Treating the accounts as entirely independent would overlook a relevant influence. The appropriate response is to clarify the information history and seek other evidence, not to accuse both of lying.

Specialised memory-support procedures should not encourage invention. Asking someone to imagine what an unseen person might have done can produce a plausible narrative without establishing an observation. Therapeutic exploration and evidence gathering also have different purposes. A meaningful image or feeling in therapy is not proof of a historical event and should not be presented as such.

Corroboration involves examining independent support for a claim. Records, physical evidence and other accounts each have their own limitations, so the question is not simply whether another item agrees. How was it produced? Does it concern the same event? Could the sources have influenced each other? A careful analysis preserves what is supported and what remains uncertain. Criminal psychology contributes by explaining these processes, not by providing a shortcut that identifies truth from confidence, emotion or the coherence of a story.

MODULE 02

2.15 Profiles, base rates and false positives

A profile is a set of hypotheses about characteristics or links inferred from available information. Its usefulness depends on whether those hypotheses are specific, testable and supported. A description can sound accurate while fitting many people. Statements such as

“the person seeks control but can appear ordinary” may seem meaningful without providing a reliable basis for identifying an individual.

Base rates describe how common something is in a defined population. They matter because a feature associated with a rare outcome may also appear in many people who do not have that outcome. A method can therefore generate many false positives if the feature is common or the outcome is rare. A memorable match does not establish that the method performs well overall.

Consider a purely mathematical illustration. In a group of one thousand people, suppose ten have a particular characteristic being assessed. A procedure identifies eight of those ten but also flags ninety-nine of the remaining people. Most flagged results would then be false positives. These invented numbers illustrate the logic; they are not performance estimates for any real profiling method.

The lesson is to ask how a method was evaluated. Were predictions recorded before the outcome was known? Were there appropriate comparison groups? Were misses and false positives counted, not only successful examples? Did the method add useful information beyond what was already available? Retelling a successful case after the fact cannot answer all of these questions.

A profile should not establish guilt, justify a diagnosis or substitute for independent evidence.

Similarity among incidents can generate a question without proving a common perpetrator. A psychological description should not be inferred from appearance, community identity or a few behaviours. The practical value of quantitative reasoning is that it challenges the attraction of a convincing story. A responsible analysis asks not only whether a hypothesis fits, but how often it would fit incorrectly and what evidence could show that it is wrong.
