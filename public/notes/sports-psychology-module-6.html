Module 6 · Alcohol and drug use among athletes

.1 Why the context matters

Substance use can relate to social expectations, pain, stress, sleep difficulties, experimentation or performance pressure. A single explanation does not fit every athlete. Discussion should avoid both moral condemnation and normalisation of harm. Ask about the behaviour, the context, its consequences and the appropriate professional response.

Prevalence estimates describe a particular population and period; they should not be generalised to all athletes. A responsible statistic needs its source, sample, definition of use, period and limitations. “Athletes use substances” is too broad to explain how a specific population was studied.

6.2 Categories are not interchangeable

Alcohol, non-medical drug use, prescribed medication and prohibited performance-enhancing substances raise overlapping but different questions. A medicine can be clinically appropriate yet require an anti-doping check. A legal substance can still cause harm. A substance's regulatory status does not establish that it is safe for an individual athlete.

Do not group all pain medicines together as though they have identical dependence risks.

Opioids, anti-inflammatory medicines and other treatments have different effects and risks.

Medication decisions, withdrawal management and pain assessment belong with qualified healthcare professionals. An educator should not recommend starting, stopping or changing a medicine.

6.3 Anti-doping literacy

WADA's 2026 Prohibited List came into force on 1 January 2026. It distinguishes substances and methods prohibited at all times, in competition or in particular sports. The Monitoring

Program is separate from the prohibited list. The applicable edition and rules must be checked for the date of competition.

For a real medication or supplement question, use the relevant anti-doping organisation and qualified medical advice. Therapeutic-use exemption processes, route of administration and other conditions cannot be resolved through a classroom slogan. A general description of the rules cannot establish that a particular product is appropriate or permitted.

6.4 Supportive first responses and referral

A first conversation can be clear without being accusatory: “You said the drinking is affecting sleep and you are worried about it. What help would be useful?” Explain the actual privacy arrangements before collecting sensitive details. Avoid promising secrecy that the service cannot provide.

Urgent intoxication, suspected overdose, severe withdrawal or immediate safety concerns require emergency or medical pathways. Motivational discussion is not a substitute for medical care. Longer-term support may involve a coordinated plan for mental health, pain, substance use and practical pressures. The appropriate services depend on assessment, not on a coach's diagnosis.

6.5 Prevention as an organisational task

Education is only one component. A team culture can also make it easier to decline alcohol, report pain, seek care and ask about medication. Review whether celebrations, rewards or selection practices create pressure to hide problems. A policy should explain responsibilities and support rather than rely only on punishment.

Case study · A question about pain relief

A fictional runner asks a trainee whether a teammate's medicine will help them compete despite pain. The trainee should not recommend the medicine or decide that it is permitted. The useful response is to direct the runner to the treating professional and the appropriate anti-doping verification route, while recognising that competing through unassessed pain may be unsafe.

MODULE 06

6.6 Understanding ambivalence and help-seeking

Ambivalence means holding competing reasons for and against a change. An athlete may dislike the effects of drinking on sleep while valuing the social connection of post-match celebrations. They may want help with pain while fearing that disclosure will affect selection.

Calling this denial or lack of discipline can obscure the actual conflict. A useful first conversation makes the competing concerns understandable without endorsing harmful behaviour.

Begin with the person's own account. Ask what they notice, what concerns them and what they would like to be different. Reflection can acknowledge both sides: “The celebrations help you feel included, and you are worried about how you feel the next morning.” This is not approval or a covert argument. It shows that the helper has understood the reason change may be difficult.

A fictional player, Kabir, says he wants to reduce drinking but expects teammates to mock him.

A purely individual plan might ask him to practise refusing. A fuller account also examines the team culture and available support. Could celebrations include alcohol-free options without making one player explain private reasons? Is there someone appropriate to approach about pressure? The athlete's choice and privacy remain central.

Questions about dependence, withdrawal, medication interactions or acute intoxication require suitable healthcare assessment. A supportive conversation should not become an improvised detoxification plan. Similarly, a team rule does not resolve every clinical need.

Punishment alone may discourage disclosure when an athlete fears losing both participation and support. The exact service response depends on its responsibilities and the circumstances.

A realistic next step may be agreeing to contact a qualified service, preparing questions or identifying a trusted support person. The helper should distinguish the intention to seek help from completed access. Review whether the person could make contact, whether the service was suitable and what obstacles remain. Respectful engagement does not guarantee change, but it can make a concern discussable without requiring the athlete to accept a moral label before receiving assistance.

MODULE 06

6.7 Pain, medication and performance pressure

Pain, treatment decisions and competitive demands can become entangled. An athlete may describe a medicine as a way to remain available for selection rather than as part of a healthcare plan. A teammate's positive experience can make borrowing a medicine seem ordinary. Psychological understanding is useful here because it identifies expectations, pressures and decision-making conditions; it does not authorise an educator to recommend the product.

Separate three questions. Is there a health problem requiring assessment? Is a treatment appropriate for this person? Is the substance or method permitted under the rules that apply to the competition? These questions can overlap but are not interchangeable. A product may be sold legally without being appropriate for an individual, and a prescribed treatment may still require the relevant anti-doping process.

In a fictional running club, Leena worries that reporting pain will be interpreted as weakness.

She considers using a teammate's medicine before an event. A useful response acknowledges the selection pressure while directing the medication and injury questions to the treating professionals. The club should also examine whether its communication makes reporting symptoms feel unsafe. A statement such as “Real athletes push through” can affect decisions even when no one explicitly instructs medicine misuse.

Prevention therefore involves more than a list of prohibited substances. It includes access to qualified advice, clear responsibility for checking questions, realistic information about uncertainty and a culture in which athletes can report concerns. Staff should not imply that a supplement is safe merely because it is familiar, natural or endorsed by a successful athlete.

Product-specific decisions require appropriate current verification.

An educational case review can identify who needs to be involved, what information is missing and which pressure is influencing the decision. It should not instruct learners to select doses, substitute medicines or circumvent testing. The psychological contribution is to support a more considered, informed process and to recognise organisational conditions that can encourage concealment. Performance ambitions matter, but they should not make health information or professional responsibilities disappear from the conversation.
