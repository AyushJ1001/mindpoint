The Mind Point · Psychological First Aid

Module 1 · Introduction to psychological first aid

.1 What psychological first aid is

Psychological first aid, abbreviated PFA, is humane, supportive and practical assistance offered to people affected by a seriously distressing event. Its purpose is to respond to immediate needs while respecting dignity, culture and abilities. It is not a diagnostic interview, a demand to discuss trauma or a shortened version of psychotherapy.

The Look–Listen–Link framework connects safety, respectful communication and practical support. Learning about PFA does not authorise a student to enter an unsafe scene, conduct an independent suicide assessment or provide medical treatment. Actual response work requires an appropriate role, preparation, coordination and referral arrangements.

A person may need a phone, a safe place to wait, information about a family member or help finding medication more urgently than a discussion of feelings. Practical assistance is not an inferior form of psychological support. It can restore access to choices that distress and disruption have made difficult.

1.2 Historical development without a single-origin story

Responses to war, disaster, bereavement and community crises influenced the development of immediate psychological support. These influences help explain why immediate support became important, but they should not be treated as a simple chain in which one event invented modern PFA. Historical crisis interventions differed in purpose, evidence and the degree of choice given to people receiving them.

Two important published reference points are the National Child Traumatic Stress Network and

National Center for PTSD field guide, whose second edition was published in 2006, and the

WHO, War Trauma Foundation and World Vision International guide published in 2011. The former describes eight core actions; the latter uses a practical framework that includes Look,

Listen and Link. Their terminology should not be mixed into an invented single official protocol.

PFA is commonly described as evidence-informed. That is not a promise that a particular conversation will prevent post-traumatic stress disorder. Helpful intentions, international endorsement and a person's immediate relief are different kinds of information from a controlled treatment outcome.

1.3 Preparation comes before contact

Preparation includes knowing your role, the situation, the response structure, available resources and the limits of what you can safely do. A helper should know how to contact the coordinating team, where medical support is located, how safeguarding concerns are escalated and which information can be shared. An improvised promise can create more difficulty than a careful admission of uncertainty.

Check practical access. Can the person hear the explanation? Is an interpreter needed? Is the location physically accessible? Does the person have a preferred support person? Is there a safe place for a conversation? These questions should not delay urgent medical assistance, but they shape ordinary supportive contact.

Example · A helper without a verified resource

A fictional volunteer has heard that a shelter has spare beds. Rather than promising a family accommodation, the volunteer checks with the coordinating desk. The shelter is full, but another site may accept arrivals after registration. Accurate uncertainty is more useful than confident misinformation. The helper can explain what is known, what is being checked and who will provide the next update.

1.4 Look: safety and immediate priorities

Look means attending to the environment and the person's needs. Notice hazards, urgent medical concerns, people separated from support and barriers to basic necessities. Do not enter a dangerous area or attempt a rescue beyond your training. Follow the authorised emergency response.

Visible distress is one clue, not a complete prioritisation system. Someone who is quiet may need urgent help; someone who is crying may be able to make choices and communicate clearly. Age or disability should not automatically be equated with incapacity. Ask what assistance is needed and recognise individual strengths.

Physical and psychological explanations can overlap. Confusion, unusual breathing, collapse or inability to respond should not be assumed to be “just stress”. A helper's task is to recognise the need for medical or specialist support, not to establish the cause through a classroom checklist.

1.5 Listen: respectful contact and present needs

Introduce yourself, explain the role and ask whether help would be welcome. Use a clear, nonintrusive opening: “I am helping at the information desk. Would it be useful to check what support you need right now?” A person can decline a conversation. Respecting that choice does not prevent offering information about how to access help later.

Listen for what matters now. The person does not need to recount the event in detail to deserve assistance. Avoid repeated questioning, pressure to express emotion or suggestions about what they must be feeling. Silence may be a choice, fatigue, a language issue or distress; its meaning should not be presumed.

Validation acknowledges experience without confirming every interpretation. “You are worried because you have not heard from your family” is different from “Something terrible must have happened to them.” The first reflects the concern; the second adds an unsupported conclusion.

1.6 Link: practical support and continuity

Link means helping the person connect with appropriate information, practical resources and chosen social support. This may involve medical care, shelter, transport, accessible communication, a trusted person or a specialist service. Ask permission where appropriate and provide the minimum information needed for the agreed purpose.

A referral is more than handing over a phone number. Can the person use the number? Is the service open? Is it accessible and appropriate? Who will confirm the next step? In an urgent situation, routine referral should not replace an emergency response. In a non-urgent situation, an agreed follow-up can identify whether access actually occurred.

1.7 Ethics, privacy and cultural humility

Respect involves accurate information, choice, non-discrimination and protection from exploitation. Do not use a crisis interaction to recruit clients, collect promotional stories or demonstrate your own expertise. A person's distress is not consent to photographs, recording or teaching use.

Explain privacy realistically. Avoid promises of absolute secrecy when safety, safeguarding or applicable law may require action. At the same time, a busy response setting does not justify unrestricted sharing. Discuss necessary information with appropriate people, not in a public group chat or casual conversation.

Culture is not a script that tells you what every member of a community wants. Ask about language, forms of address, spiritual support, privacy and family involvement. A cultural or religious resource may be helpful when the person chooses it; it should not replace necessary medical care or be imposed because of appearance or background.

MODULE 01

1.8 Five connected aims of immediate support

Immediate support can be understood through five connected aims: improving safety, supporting manageable calm, strengthening a sense of capability, maintaining connection and offering realistic hope. These aims help explain why practical actions can have psychological value. They are not five compulsory exercises or stages that every person must complete. The situation and the person's priorities determine what is useful.

Safety concerns actual conditions as well as the person's understanding of those conditions. A person may need reliable information about an evacuation area, protection from an ongoing threat or access to medical care. Calm does not mean removing every emotion. It may mean reducing unnecessary noise, slowing the delivery of information or giving someone enough space to think. An upset person can still understand, choose and act.

A sense of capability can be supported by helping the person carry out an action they choose.

A helper might locate a charging point so the person can make their own call, rather than taking over the conversation. Connection can involve family, friends, community resources or another trusted person, but the person should choose who is appropriate. A family relationship is not automatically safe or supportive.

Realistic hope concerns available possibilities rather than promises. “The transport desk is checking the next vehicle” is grounded in an action. “Everything will be back to normal tomorrow” claims knowledge the helper may not have. Hope can coexist with uncertainty, grief or a serious practical problem. It does not require optimism about every outcome.

In a fictional reception centre, Asha wants to contact her mother but cannot hear announcements clearly. A helper offers written information and checks the phone facilities.

This small sequence supports access, capability and connection. It does not require Asha to describe the distressing event or become visibly calm. The aims are useful because they direct attention toward what the interaction makes possible. They should be reviewed through the person's experience and the practical result, not through an assumption that the helper has completed a psychological treatment.

MODULE 01

1.9 Preparing a usable response role

Preparation begins before approaching an affected person. A helper needs to know where they are expected to work, who coordinates the response, what assistance is available and which concerns require immediate escalation. The role should be specific enough to explain in a sentence. “I help people reach the information desk” is clearer than “I am here to take care of everything.”

A prepared helper distinguishes confirmed information from unverified reports. A shelter may appear on a directory but be full. A clinic may be open but unable to meet a particular need. A telephone number may work while remaining inaccessible to someone who cannot use the language of the recorded message. Preparation includes knowing how to verify these details and how to report gaps.

Consider a fictional community hall receiving families after an evacuation. Before beginning, the team agrees where medical staff can be reached, who handles safeguarding, how interpreters are requested and who provides public updates. Volunteers are told not to collect full personal histories or promise accommodation. They practise a short handover so that a concern does not depend on one person's memory.

Practical readiness also includes the helper's own condition. Fatigue, personal distress, unclear instructions or lack of necessary equipment can affect the quality of support. Asking for clarification or a break is preferable to improvising beyond the role. Preparation is a shared organisational responsibility, not evidence that a volunteer should personally anticipate every possible event.

The plan must remain adaptable. If circumstances change, the team updates roles and information rather than continuing with an obsolete arrangement. A useful briefing identifies what changed, who needs to know and which previous instructions no longer apply. In ordinary educational practice, learners can analyse this process using a fictional setting. The central lesson is that supportive contact rests on reliable organisation. Warmth matters, but warmth without accurate information, clear responsibility and access to appropriate help can leave the person with another problem to solve.

MODULE 01

1.10 Making first contact without intruding

First contact should give the person enough information to decide whether they want assistance. Introduce yourself, describe your role and ask a simple permission question. The opening should fit the setting and language. “I am helping with information here. Would you like assistance with anything right now?” leaves more choice than beginning with a series of questions about feelings or the event.

A person's answer can be direct, uncertain or absent. Silence does not always mean refusal, and it does not always mean a psychological problem. They may not hear, may need an interpreter, may be occupied or may want privacy. Check the communication channel respectfully. Do not crowd the person or repeat the same question more loudly as though volume alone will resolve uncertainty.

In a fictional waiting area, Ravi turns away when a volunteer approaches. The volunteer says,

“You do not have to talk with me. The information desk is beside the entrance.” Ravi then asks whether there is a place to sit with his brother. The useful response concerns seating and contact, not why he initially turned away. Respecting a boundary can make a practical request easier to express.

Permission also applies within the interaction. Agreeing to help with transport does not mean agreeing to photographs, a public account, physical touch or contact with relatives. Before changing the task, explain what you propose and ask where appropriate. A helper should not assume that distress removes the person's ability to make choices.

The opening should not become a rigid script. If there is an urgent medical or safety concern, the authorised emergency response takes priority. Otherwise, a clear role, a manageable question and room for correction provide a useful beginning. Good first contact is measured by whether the person understands the offer and can respond freely. It is not measured by how quickly the helper obtains a personal story, produces visible emotion or persuades someone to accept a particular kind of support.

MODULE 01

1.11 Practical support, counselling and clinical care

Practical support, counselling and clinical care can all matter after a distressing event, but they serve different purposes. Practical support helps someone obtain information, contact, shelter, transport or another immediate resource. Counselling may provide a continuing setting for understanding difficulties, coping, decisions or adjustment. Clinical assessment and treatment address defined health concerns through appropriately qualified processes.

Psychological first aid is usually concerned with immediate, humane support and connection. It does not require a diagnosis. A person who wants help charging a phone does not need to demonstrate symptoms before receiving assistance. Conversely, a person with a serious health concern should not be kept in a supportive conversation when urgent care is needed. The role is guided by need, not by a preference for talking.

In a fictional example, three people arrive after a building evacuation. One needs directions to a verified transport point. Another wants a quiet place while waiting for information. A third is confused and physically unwell. The first may need clear practical information, the second may welcome ordinary company, and the third requires the appropriate medical pathway. Identical breathing exercises for all three would ignore these differences.

Support can change over time. A person may initially focus on documents and later seek counselling. Another may not want psychological services at all. Persistent distress, disruption to functioning or specific safety concerns may indicate a need for further assessment, but this should not be assumed for everyone exposed to an event. Neither visible calm nor visible sadness establishes the entire picture.

A helpful handover states the need and the limits of what has been done. “I helped the person reach the medical desk” differs from “I treated their panic.” Accurate language supports continuity and avoids a misleading account of the service. The distinction is not a hierarchy in which practical help is less important. A coherent response matches the contribution to the need and recognises when several kinds of assistance must work together without asking one helper to perform every role.

MODULE 01

1.12 Reliable information when the situation is uncertain

Uncertainty can be distressing, especially when it concerns family, safety or access to basic needs. A helper may feel pressure to answer quickly, but confidence is not a substitute for accuracy. Useful information distinguishes what is confirmed, what remains unknown and how an update will be obtained. A clear statement of uncertainty is often more helpful than an encouraging guess.

The source of information matters. An official announcement, a direct confirmation from the responsible team and a message forwarded through several people have different levels of reliability. Repetition does not create independent confirmation. A rumour that appears in several group chats may still come from one unverified claim. Helpers should avoid becoming another link in that chain.

In a fictional evacuation centre, someone says a road has reopened. Families begin preparing to leave, but the coordination desk has not confirmed safe access. A volunteer should not announce the rumour as fact. A useful response is, “I have heard that report, but it has not been confirmed here. I will check with the transport coordinator.” The response states both the limitation and the next action.

Information should also be usable. A long explanation may overwhelm someone who needs one immediate decision. Offer the most relevant point first, use plain language and check understanding. Written or translated information may help. Avoid treating a nod as proof of comprehension, particularly in a noisy environment or when the person is distressed.

When no new information is available, explain that honestly rather than creating an update to fill silence. Agree how the person can receive further information, without promising an exact time unless it is known. Good communication reduces avoidable confusion; it cannot remove every uncertainty. The helper's contribution is to make the information process more trustworthy and accessible. This includes correcting an earlier error promptly, stating what has changed and ensuring that the correction reaches the people who may have relied on the original message.

MODULE 01

1.13 Culture, dignity and the meaning of help

Culture influences language, expectations, family roles, spiritual practices and ways of expressing distress. It does not provide a fixed script for what every person from a community wants. A helper should use cultural knowledge as a reason to ask thoughtful questions, not as permission to make decisions on someone's behalf. Individual preferences remain important within shared traditions.

Dignity is visible in ordinary actions: asking how a person wishes to be addressed, explaining a process before beginning, preserving privacy and speaking directly to the person rather than only to companions. A need for assistance does not erase adulthood, expertise or agency. An older person or someone with a disability may know exactly what support works for them.

In a fictional relief setting, a volunteer assumes that a woman wants a religious leader contacted because of her clothing. She actually wants a female interpreter and a private place to speak with her sister. Asking about her priorities would have identified the useful support without imposing an identity-based expectation. Religious or community resources can be valuable when chosen; they should not be presumed appropriate.

Interpreters and support people also need role clarity. Interpretation should convey the person's meaning rather than substitute the interpreter's advice. Sensitive information should not be shared casually because someone speaks the same language. A child should not automatically be made responsible for interpreting distressing or private adult information. Use the service's appropriate communication arrangements.

Culturally responsive support includes examining access barriers in the environment.

Registration procedures, written forms, opening hours and assumptions about family availability may exclude people even when staff are polite. The question is not only whether the helper appears respectful but whether the person can actually use the service. A useful approach combines curiosity, clear explanation and practical adaptation. It recognises shared contexts without reducing anyone to a category, and it treats feedback about an unsuitable offer as information that can improve the response.
