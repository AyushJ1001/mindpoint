The Mind Point · Criminal Psychology

Module 1 · Understanding criminal behaviour

.1 The field and its boundaries

Criminal psychology examines psychological questions concerning offending, investigation, people affected by crime and responses to harmful behaviour. Forensic psychology is a broader area concerned with the application of psychology to legal questions. Criminology also studies crime, including social patterns, institutions and systems. Their topics overlap, but their methods and professional roles are not identical.

An explanation of behaviour is not a finding of guilt. A psychological assessment is not a substitute for an investigation. A diagnosis is not a legal conclusion about responsibility. These distinctions remain important throughout the course. A person suspected or accused of an offence should not be described as though the allegation has already been established.

The introductory learner's task is to understand concepts, evaluate claims and recognise professional limits. It is not to profile classmates, investigate neighbours, question suspects or use psychological labels to resolve a disagreement.

1.3 Biological and historical theories

Lombroso’s atavistic theory and Sheldon’s somatotype theory are historical ideas to examine critically, not accepted tools for identifying offenders. Inferring criminality from facial features, skull shape, body build, ancestry or community identity is not a defensible assessment method.

A historical classification can reveal the assumptions of its period without providing reliable evidence about an individual.

Biological research asks more precise questions about development, regulation and interactions with environments. Even where a group-level association is found, it does not determine a person's actions or legal responsibility. A risk factor is not destiny, and association is not proof of causation.

A sound explanation identifies what was measured, who was studied, alternative explanations and the limits of generalisation. Appearance-based claims do not establish why a particular person acted as they did.

1.4 Developmental pathways

Moffitt's developmental taxonomy distinguishes adolescence-limited and life-course-persistent patterns of antisocial behaviour. It proposes different developmental processes rather than two diagnoses to assign from one incident. The taxonomy describes developmental patterns, not a prediction for a particular child.

A developmental account asks when a pattern began, where it occurs, how it changes and which protective relationships or opportunities are present. The same act can occur within different histories. Conversely, a difficult childhood does not mean that offending will follow.

Example · Two incomplete histories

Two fictional adolescents are reported to have participated in property damage. One has no previous reported incidents; the other has a longer history of school conflict. The information does not establish a developmental pathway, diagnosis or future course for either person. A careful response asks about evidence, circumstances, development, support and the young person's account. It also preserves the difference between an allegation and an established act.

1.5 Psychological theories: different questions

Psychodynamic explanations examine conflict, meaning and developmental experience. They can generate hypotheses, but a claim about unconscious motives is not established merely because it sounds plausible. Do not infer undisclosed trauma or hidden hostility from one act.

Behavioural explanations examine antecedents, consequences and learning. A behaviour may be maintained by a reward, escape from a demand or social approval. Its function requires evidence; it cannot be assumed from its appearance. Reinforcement describes an effect on behaviour, not a moral approval of it.

Social learning explanations ask how behaviour is modelled and how observed consequences influence learning. Exposure to a model does not guarantee imitation. A person can encounter harmful behaviour without adopting it. The relevant questions include context, alternatives, opportunities and consequences.

Cognitive explanations examine interpretations, decision processes and justifications. A person may minimise harm or shift responsibility, but those claims should be assessed from actual statements and behaviour rather than attributed automatically to everyone accused of an offence.

Personality approaches examine patterns of traits and functioning. A trait is not a conviction, and a clinical diagnosis is not a complete explanation of offending. Avoid using psychopathy, narcissism or other labels as insults or as substitutes for evidence.

Applying a theory to a real setting requires relevant evidence, careful definitions and attention to competing explanations. A theory can organise questions without establishing conclusions about every person in a community.

1.7 Risk, protection and context

A useful formulation considers both risks and protective resources. Family support, education, safe housing, healthcare and opportunities can matter alongside individual functioning. Do not reduce a person to poverty, migration status, family structure or a diagnosis. A single-parent family is not inherently a cause of offending; the relevant circumstances need to be examined.

Separate explanations of an act from needs for support. A person can need mental health care without that need explaining an offence. A person can be accountable for harm while also needing housing or treatment. These questions can be considered together without collapsing them into one label.

1.8 Reading a claim about crime

Ask what the claim is based on: an allegation, a conviction, an interview, a clinical assessment, a study or an opinion. Then ask whether the conclusion goes beyond that evidence. A vivid case is not a prevalence study. A correlation is not a causal mechanism. A news report is not a complete psychological assessment.

A fictional case can illustrate reasoning, but evidence about a real case must be checked independently. Numerical claims need a clearly defined sample, timeframe and method.

MODULE 01

1.9 Levels of explanation: person, situation and social context

An explanation of offending can operate at several levels. Individual-level questions concern learning, cognition, emotion and development. Situational questions concern the immediate setting, opportunities and interactions. Social-level questions concern institutions, resources and patterns across communities. These levels can be connected, but evidence at one level should not automatically be treated as proof at another.

A study of recorded incidents in an area does not identify which resident will offend. A clinical assessment of one person does not establish the cause of a population trend. A theory about social pressure does not show why a particular person acted as they did. Good reasoning keeps the unit of analysis clear: who or what was studied, what was measured and which conclusion follows.

In a fictional example, equipment disappears from several offices in a business district. An area-level analysis might examine access arrangements and reporting practices. An investigation of a particular incident might examine records, witness accounts and physical evidence. A psychological assessment, if appropriate and authorised, would answer a defined question about a person. These are different tasks, not interchangeable routes to the same conclusion.

Association also differs from causation. Two variables may occur together because one influences the other, because both are influenced by another factor or because of how the data were collected. A finding that a particular difficulty is more common in one sample does not establish that it causes offending. Definitions, comparison groups and alternative explanations matter.

An introductory analysis should therefore state the level of the claim and the missing information. It can consider several explanations without treating them as equally supported.

The aim is not to avoid conclusions indefinitely, but to make conclusions proportionate to the evidence. Criminal psychology becomes more useful when it connects personal functioning with situations and systems while resisting the temptation to explain an offence through one trait, one diagnosis or one feature of a person's background.

MODULE 01

1.10 Learning, consequences and a fictional behavioural sequence

Behavioural explanations examine what happens before and after an action and how those consequences may influence future behaviour. An antecedent is a preceding event or condition. A consequence follows the behaviour. Reinforcement refers to a consequence that increases the likelihood of the behaviour in relevant conditions; it does not mean that the behaviour is morally acceptable.

The appearance of an action does not reveal its function. Leaving a room might avoid an unwanted demand, protect someone from danger or follow an instruction. The same action can have different consequences for different people. A functional account is a hypothesis supported by information about the sequence, not a label assigned from a brief description.

In a fictional case, a young person sends insulting messages after feeling excluded from a group. Peers respond with attention and approval. One possible maintaining process is that this response makes similar messages more likely. Another possibility is that the person uses the messages to end an uncomfortable conversation. The available account needs clarification about what happened, what the person expected and which consequences followed repeatedly.

Understanding a function does not excuse harm. The recipients' experience and appropriate accountability remain important. A support plan can consider alternative ways to seek connection, respond to exclusion or manage conflict without suggesting that the harmful action was unavoidable. The environment also matters: a peer group that rewards humiliation contributes a different context from one that discourages it.

A useful educational formulation separates the observed act, possible antecedents, immediate consequences, later costs and alternative responses. Each uncertain part should remain tentative. Intervention planning with a real person requires the appropriate assessment and professional role. The conceptual lesson is that learning theory asks how behaviour is acquired and maintained, not whether a person is inherently good or bad. It can make change more specific by identifying actions and conditions that might be altered, while keeping responsibility, safety and the limits of the evidence visible.

1.11 Developmental pathways and protective processes

A developmental explanation asks how patterns emerge and change over time. It considers early functioning, relationships, learning opportunities, adverse experiences and transitions.

The aim is to understand a course of development rather than infer a future from one incident.

Moffitt's developmental taxonomy distinguishes adolescence-limited and life-course-persistent patterns as theoretical accounts of different pathways, not as diagnoses that can be assigned during a short conversation.

Longitudinal information is important because the timing and persistence of behaviour matter.

An isolated allegation, a period of conflict and a repeated pattern across years are different forms of evidence. Even a documented history does not make the future fixed. Changes in relationships, opportunity, treatment, education or circumstances can alter what happens next.

Protective processes deserve attention alongside risks. A trusted adult, a stable educational setting, a meaningful activity or access to appropriate care can support development. A protective factor is not a guarantee, just as a risk factor is not destiny. Its relevance depends on whether it is actually available, usable and meaningful in the person's life.

In a fictional case, Imran has changed schools and is accused of threatening another student.

The account also mentions a supportive teacher and an interest in repairing bicycles. These details should not be turned into a predetermined rehabilitation story. They are possible resources to explore while the allegation, immediate safety and communication needs are addressed through appropriate procedures.

A developmental formulation should avoid treating poverty, a single-parent family, migration or a diagnosis as an automatic cause of offending. The relevant circumstances need to be described precisely. It should also avoid predicting lifelong dangerousness from a child's behaviour or an informal checklist. The practical value of developmental thinking is that it expands the questions: what has changed, what keeps the difficulty going, which supports exist and what opportunities are missing? It supports a more individualised account while preserving the distinction between explaining possible influences and establishing legal responsibility.

1.12 Comparing theories without forcing one complete story

Different theories direct attention to different parts of an event. A cognitive account may examine interpretations and justifications. A social-learning account may examine models and observed consequences. A routine-activity account may examine opportunity and guardianship. A strain perspective may examine pressures and blocked opportunities. These approaches need not be treated as a competition in which one name automatically explains every case.

A useful comparison asks what each theory predicts, what evidence would support it and what it leaves unexplained. A theory is not supported merely because a story can be retold using its vocabulary. It should generate questions that can be examined. Evidence that challenges the account should lead to revision rather than being reinterpreted automatically as further proof.

Consider a fictional incident of property damage after a group disagreement. A cognitive question concerns what participants believed was happening and whether they justified the damage. A learning question concerns peer responses and previous consequences. A situational question concerns access and supervision. A developmental question concerns whether the event forms part of a broader pattern. None establishes guilt from the vignette alone.

Theories also differ in the level at which they operate. A social explanation of recorded patterns should not become a diagnosis of an individual. A person's account of anger should not become proof that anger caused the offence. The evidence may support several interacting contributors, or it may be insufficient to distinguish them. A clear analysis names that limitation instead of filling the gap with a dramatic motive.

The goal is a coherent, revisable explanation tied to information. It can recognise harm and accountability while also considering context and support needs. It should not reduce an entire community to a theoretical category or treat a diagnosis as an explanation of criminality.

Comparing theories carefully develops a central skill: asking which question a model helps answer and which conclusion still requires independent evidence. This is more valuable than memorising a list of theorists and attaching one to every event.
