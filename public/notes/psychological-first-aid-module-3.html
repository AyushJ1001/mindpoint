The Mind Point · Psychological First Aid

Module 3 · Supporting people, helpers and communities

.1 Loss, uncertainty and immediate priorities

Loss may concern a person, home, livelihood, routine, role or anticipated future. A helper should acknowledge the person's meaning rather than decide which loss counts as serious.

Grief does not follow a timetable that a PFA provider can enforce. The immediate task may be to arrange contact, obtain information or identify a place to rest.

Uncertainty can be painful without being resolved by reassurance. Distinguish what is confirmed, what is not known and when a further update may be available. Avoid repeating rumours. When no new information is available, saying so clearly can preserve trust better than offering a guess.

A person may want to speak, remain quiet or focus on a task. Writing, drawing or other expressive activities should not become requirements. In an emergency, an invitation to create a detailed trauma narrative may be intrusive and unnecessary. Present-focused practical support remains a legitimate choice.

3.2 Needs assessment without an overwhelming interview

Ask about urgent needs, available support and barriers. A short conversation may identify that the person has a phone but no charge, a referral but no transport, or medication information but no access to a clinician. The barrier often determines whether a resource is usable.

Do not collect a complete life history when the purpose is to arrange one practical service.

Record only what is required by the role and organisation. In a classroom exercise, use fictional names and circumstances rather than actual client information.

3.3 Linking and a closed-loop handover

A closed-loop handover means checking that the next person or service has received the request and that responsibility is clear. It does not mean guaranteeing access that is beyond your control. State the purpose, relevant facts, consent or other lawful sharing basis, actions taken and the next decision needed.

A fictional example: “The person would like help contacting an accessible transport service.

They have agreed that I share their current location and mobility requirement with the coordination desk. No medical assessment has been made by me. Please confirm whether transport is available and who will update them.” This is more useful than “Please help; they are very upset.”

For urgent concerns, use the immediate escalation route rather than waiting for a routine email response. For non-urgent support, agree realistic follow-up and explain what happens if the service cannot be reached. A person should not leave believing that a referral has been accepted when it has only been suggested.

3.4 Children and safeguarding

Children may need developmentally appropriate information, a familiar safe caregiver and help maintaining ordinary routines. Their reactions can vary with age, communication abilities and circumstances. Do not interpret every change in play or behaviour as proof of a specific traumatic experience.

Family reunification must follow authorised safeguarding procedures. Do not hand a child to an adult simply because that adult claims a relationship. Do not promise a reunion time that is unknown. A helper should know the designated safeguarding lead and report concerns through the appropriate pathway.

Use clear, short explanations and offer choices that are genuinely available. “Would you like to sit near the desk or beside this quieter area while we wait?” may be suitable when both options are safe and supervised. Avoid graphic details, questioning designed to investigate an allegation or pressure to draw what happened. Specialist child protection and clinical work require appropriate competence.

3.5 Older adults, disability and communication access

Support should recognise abilities as well as needs. Ask before assisting with mobility, communication or personal belongings. An older adult may be an important source of local knowledge; a person with a disability may have an established way of managing emergencies.

Do not replace their judgement merely because assistance is needed.

New confusion or physical changes can require medical assessment. Do not assume that confusion is normal ageing or an emotional reaction. Ask about access to essential aids and medical support within the relevant role, without changing medication or making a diagnosis.

Accessible communication can include large print, a quiet space, an interpreter, written instructions or extra time. A person who does not respond to spoken questions may not have heard or understood them. Check the communication channel before assuming lack of cooperation.

3.6 Inclusion and barriers to support

Marginalisation, language, poverty and discrimination raise questions about access, not assumptions about a whole group. An apparently available service can remain inaccessible because of documentation requirements, cost, transport, safety, privacy or mistrust.

Ask which barriers are relevant to the person. Community organisations can help identify practical solutions, but membership in a group does not justify sharing someone's information with its leaders. Choice and confidentiality still matter.

A resource map should include ordinary supports as well as specialist services: transport, food access, interpretation, healthcare, legal assistance and safe accommodation. A psychological explanation should not obscure a material problem that requires action.

3.7 Helper stress and organisational support

Helpers can experience exhaustion, distress and reactions to others' suffering. Secondary traumatic stress, compassion fatigue and burnout are related but not interchangeable concepts. Secondary traumatic stress concerns trauma-related reactions associated with indirect exposure; burnout concerns sustained occupational strain. A label does not replace assessment of a helper's actual needs.

Prevention and response cannot be reduced to individual self-care. Workload, rest, staffing, role clarity, supervision, safety and the ability to ask for help are organisational responsibilities.

A helper who needs a break should not have to demonstrate a dramatic crisis before receiving support.

An operational review can examine what happened, what resources were missing and what should change. Voluntary supportive discussion is different from compulsory emotional recounting. Do not require everyone to describe traumatic details in a group as a condition of being considered resilient.

3.8 Community resilience and preparedness

Community resilience concerns resources and relationships that help people respond to disruption and rebuild. It should not imply that a community must manage without services or that people are to blame when resources are inadequate. Practical infrastructure and fair access matter alongside coping skills.

Preparedness can include an updated contact directory, clear response roles, accessible information, backup communication and coordination with local services. Training should use fictional, proportionate simulations rather than surprising learners with graphic crisis enactments. A briefing should explain the task, limits and right to stop.

Public education should be evaluated modestly. Attendance shows reach, not necessarily improved mental health. Useful indicators might include whether participants can locate a verified service, whether a referral route works and whether information is accessible. Do not claim that a campaign reduced illness without evidence that can support that conclusion.

3.9 Building a resource network

A network connects people and organisations with complementary responsibilities. Begin with a needs map, identify existing resources, confirm eligibility and access, agree communication and review gaps. A directory should record who checked each service and when. A contact can become outdated; a printed list is not a permanent guarantee.

A practical record includes service name, purpose, hours, eligibility, language and accessibility, contact route, cost where relevant, last verification date and a backup. Sensitive personal details should not appear in a public resource directory. Review responsibility should belong to an identified team rather than “someone”.

Linking to long-term recovery may involve housing, education, medical care, livelihood support and specialist mental health services. PFA providers do not need to deliver every service themselves. A coherent contribution includes knowing where the role ends and who can take responsibility next.

3.10 Case study: flooding in a community

In this fictional case, several families arrive at a reception centre after evacuation. One person wants information about relatives, another has lost access to essential medication, and a third wants a quiet place with their children. No diagnosis is supplied or inferred.

The team first checks safety and medical priorities. It identifies a medical contact for the medication concern, explains the family information process and checks available space. A helper asks permission before contacting a relative. The person who wants quiet is not required to join a sharing circle.

A complication occurs: the proposed transport service is unavailable. The helper updates the family rather than pretending the referral succeeded. The coordinating team looks for an alternative and states when it will report back. The outcome is recorded as an access problem being addressed, not as proof that everyone has emotionally recovered.

The teaching point is coordination. A calm conversation can be useful, but it does not replace medication access, reliable information or safe accommodation. PFA is part of a broader response.

3.11 Case study: a school evacuation

In this fictional school evacuation, Students and staff have reached an approved gathering area after an alarming incident. A student is quiet and wants to contact a caregiver; another is breathing unusually and looks unwell; a teacher asks everyone to describe their experience immediately.

The response distinguishes needs. The student seeking contact can be helped through the school's verified reunification procedure. The unwell student requires the medical response pathway rather than an assumption of panic. The proposed compulsory recounting should be replaced with clear information, optional support and appropriate follow-up.

Staff should avoid releasing photographs or identifying details in informal messages.

Information to families should come through authorised channels. Later support can be offered without assuming that every student will develop a disorder or that those who appear calm need nothing.

Worked example · A referral that was sent but not completed

In a fictional relief centre, Rupa agrees to contact a service about replacing essential documents. A volunteer provides a telephone number and records “referred.” The next day,

Rupa has not made contact. The number is working, but she has no private place to call and the recorded message is in a language she does not understand. Counting the referral as a completed connection would hide the barrier.

The helper first checks what support Rupa wants, rather than assuming unwillingness. Possible next steps include finding an approved interpreter, arranging an appropriate time to call or identifying an accessible alternative through the response coordinator. The helper should not promise that documents will be issued, take unnecessary copies of private records or make decisions on Rupa's behalf.

A better handover records the need, her agreement, the access barrier, the proposed next step and who will check whether contact occurred. Only relevant information should travel with it.

When a service cannot accept a referral, that fact should be communicated honestly and another appropriate route considered. The worked example makes “Link” concrete: a list of contacts is a resource, but an effective connection also depends on availability, access, consent and responsibility for the next action.

3.12 Consolidation and responsible next steps

A strong introductory response can describe what is known, notice what requires escalation, ask about the person's priorities and identify an appropriate next step. It does not need a diagnostic label or a dramatic intervention.

Use the companion workbook to practise first contact, resource verification and handover.

These simulations build understanding, not independent emergency competence. Before any real response role, confirm training, supervision, local procedures and the limits of responsibility.

MODULE 03

3.13 Supporting children without investigating their experience

Children may need familiar routines, developmentally appropriate information and access to a safe caregiver. Their reactions can include questions, quietness, play, irritability or a wish to return to an ordinary activity. A helper should not assume that a child must discuss the event or draw a picture of it to receive support. Behaviour has several possible meanings and should be understood in context.

Communication should be clear and concrete. Explain what is known and what will happen next without adding graphic detail or making promises. “We are waiting here while the school checks who can collect you” is more accurate than promising a reunion time that has not been confirmed. Repeating a short explanation may be helpful; repeated questioning about the event may not be.

In a fictional school reception area, a child asks the same question about a caregiver several times. The helper answers consistently, checks whether the child understood and offers an ordinary activity while waiting. The aim is not to distract the child from every feeling. It is to support a manageable waiting period while the authorised reunification process continues.

Safeguarding is different from informal reassurance. A person claiming to be a relative must be checked through the appropriate procedures. If a child discloses possible harm, the helper should respond calmly, avoid leading questions and follow the safeguarding pathway. The helper should not conduct an independent investigation, promise absolute secrecy or ask the child to repeat the account to several unnecessary listeners.

Age, disability, language and individual preferences affect what support is accessible. A child who communicates differently may need an established communication method or an appropriate specialist. Familiarity should not become infantilisation. The central principles are to preserve safety, communicate accurately and avoid adding pressure. Support can include ordinary play, quiet company or practical information when appropriate, but these are not diagnostic tools. A drawing or a change in play does not by itself prove a particular event or psychological condition.

MODULE 03

3.14 Disability, ageing and practical access

Access is part of support, not a separate courtesy. A service may be available in principle but unusable because of stairs, noise, small print, inaccessible transport or an unsuitable communication method. The helper should ask what the person needs and what already works for them. Assistance is most respectful when it builds on the person's knowledge rather than replacing it.

Age and disability do not automatically determine capacity or dependence. An older adult may know the local area well and prefer to make their own calls. A wheelchair user may need information about a route, not someone to take control of the chair. A person with hearing differences may understand the process once it is presented in writing. Ask before touching equipment or moving personal belongings.

In a fictional relief centre, Mr Desai appears not to respond to spoken instructions. A volunteer assumes confusion. His daughter explains that his hearing aid battery is depleted. The team arranges an appropriate communication alternative and checks access to the aid. This does not rule out every health concern, but it shows why the communication channel should be examined before attributing a psychological cause.

New confusion, sudden functional change or physical symptoms can require medical attention.

They should not be dismissed as normal ageing or emotional stress. The helper can communicate what changed, when it was noticed and what support is currently available.

Medication changes and diagnosis remain with the appropriate healthcare professionals.

A practical access review considers the whole route: receiving information, reaching the service, completing any process and obtaining the next support. A ramp at the entrance does not solve an inaccessible form or an unavailable return journey. Ask whether the person wants a companion involved and respect privacy. Effective support makes choices more usable. It does not measure independence by whether someone can do everything without assistance, and it does not treat a request for help as permission to make every subsequent decision on the person's behalf.

MODULE 03

3.15 Loss, grief and uncertainty about what has happened

Loss can concern a person, a home, a role, a livelihood or a future that now seems uncertain.

The helper should not decide which losses are serious enough to deserve attention. A person may focus on an ordinary object because it represents safety, family or continuity.

Understanding that meaning does not require a complete life history or a detailed account of the event.

Grief can include sadness, anger, numbness, guilt, relief or shifting reactions. These experiences do not have to appear in a fixed order. A person who is organising practical tasks may still be grieving. A person who is crying may still be able to make decisions. Support should follow the current need rather than enforce a sequence of emotional expression.

Ambiguous loss involves uncertainty, such as not knowing a loved one's location or whether a home can be returned to. Ordinary reassurance may be particularly unhelpful when it claims an outcome that is unknown. A useful response separates hope from certainty: “The team is checking the information, and we will tell you what is confirmed.” This acknowledges the painful waiting without filling the gap with speculation.

In a fictional centre, Saira wants to retrieve a family photograph but cannot enter the affected building. The helper acknowledges its importance and explains the current access restrictions.

They explore what can be done through the authorised team without promising retrieval or encouraging an unsafe return. The photograph's emotional value does not make the physical risk disappear.

A helper can offer company, a quiet place, information or contact with chosen support.

Cultural and spiritual practices may matter, but should not be assumed from identity. Avoid requiring acceptance, forgiveness or a positive lesson. Longer-term grief support may be useful when wanted or indicated, but immediate practical assistance remains legitimate. The central task is to accompany the person's current priorities with accuracy and respect, not to resolve grief within a short interaction or demonstrate that the person has moved on.

MODULE 03

3.16 Helper stress and the organisation of support

Helpers can be affected by distressing material, uncertainty, workload and repeated exposure to other people's suffering. These experiences may involve fatigue, irritability, difficulty concentrating or changes in sleep. Secondary traumatic stress, burnout and ordinary tiredness overlap in some features but are not identical concepts. A label is less useful than understanding what the helper is experiencing and what conditions need attention.

Individual care matters, but it cannot compensate for an unsafe workload or absent support.

Organisations influence shift length, breaks, role clarity, supervision, equipment and the availability of backup. A helper should not have to reach a crisis before being allowed to rest or seek assistance. The ability to report uncertainty and mistakes without humiliation is part of a workable response system.

In a fictional team, a volunteer repeatedly stays beyond the end of a shift because they feel guilty leaving. Over time they begin forgetting handover details. The response should not be another instruction to become more resilient. The coordinator can clarify coverage, ensure a reliable transfer process and discuss manageable shifts. Personal beliefs about responsibility can be explored alongside organisational arrangements.

Operational review differs from compulsory emotional recounting. A team can examine which messages were unclear, where supplies were missing and how referrals functioned without requiring everyone to describe distressing experiences in detail. Voluntary support should be available through appropriate channels. A group discussion should not become an expectation that helpers publicly disclose personal trauma or prove that they are unaffected.

A useful review asks what support was available, what was used and what barriers remained.

Did helpers know whom to contact? Were breaks possible in practice? Were difficult decisions shared appropriately? Persistent or concerning symptoms may require professional care. The aim is sustainable, accountable support rather than an ideal of unlimited self-sacrifice. Looking after helpers also protects the quality of assistance because fatigue, unclear responsibility and fear of asking for help can affect decisions made for the people receiving care.

MODULE 03

3.17 Making a referral usable

A referral names a possible source of help, but a usable connection requires more. The service must be appropriate, available and reachable. The person needs understandable information and a realistic way to act on it. A printed number or address can be valuable, yet it does not establish that the person has received care or that responsibility has transferred.

Begin by clarifying the purpose. Is the need medical, practical, safeguarding-related or a request for continuing psychological support? Then check the service's role, eligibility, communication arrangements and access barriers. Ask what assistance the person wants.

Some people prefer to make contact independently; others may need an interpreter, transport or help understanding the process.

Rupa, the fictional learner from the earlier example, has a working telephone number but cannot use the service because the message is in an unfamiliar language and she lacks privacy.

Describing her as noncompliant would misidentify the problem. A useful plan looks for an appropriate language option and a suitable place or time to call, with her agreement. The helper does not promise that the service will issue documents or resolve every need.

A closed-loop handover checks receipt and the next action. It states relevant information, the basis for sharing it, what has already been done and who is responsible. It also records when a referral is declined or unavailable. An honest account of a gap allows the coordinating team to consider alternatives; a misleading record of completed care hides the gap.

Review should be proportionate to the role and urgency. Immediate concerns require the authorised urgent pathway, not a delayed routine message. Non-urgent contact may allow an agreed follow-up. The person should understand what happens if the first route fails. The practical measure of linking is not how many contacts a helper distributes, but whether the process supports a suitable next step while preserving privacy, choice and clarity about responsibility. Access is an ongoing part of the response rather than an assumption attached to a referral label.

MODULE 03

3.18 A community flood response: following different priorities

After flooding, a fictional community hall becomes a reception point. Several families arrive with different priorities. One adult needs information about a relative, another has lost access to essential medication and another wants an accessible place to wait with a child. The team should not assume that a group discussion of feelings is the most urgent or appropriate response for everyone.

The first task is to work within the coordinated safety arrangements. Medical concerns are directed to the responsible healthcare team. Family information is checked through verified channels. Accessibility needs are discussed with the person rather than inferred from appearance. Each action should have an identified purpose, and only necessary information should be collected or shared.

A helper approaches Leela, who is crying, and asks what would be useful. Leela explains that she knows her family is safe but cannot find her identity documents. Nearby, a quiet adult reports feeling increasingly unwell. Visible emotion does not determine priority: Leela may need practical guidance, while the unwell person needs appropriate assessment. The response follows need rather than the intensity of expression.

The situation changes when the proposed transport becomes unavailable. The team informs those affected, checks alternatives and explains what remains uncertain. It does not continue announcing the earlier plan to avoid disappointment. Another family declines contact with a relative and names a friend instead. Their choice should be respected within the relevant safety and legal responsibilities.

At review, the team records concrete outcomes: which contacts were made, which medical concerns were handed over, which access barriers remain and who will follow up. It should not claim that everyone has recovered emotionally because the hall became quieter. The case illustrates how psychological first aid fits into a broader response. Respectful conversation, practical support and reliable organisation reinforce one another. No single helper needs to solve every problem, but each should make their contribution clear enough that the person is not left to navigate a confusing gap between services.

MODULE 03

3.19 Preparedness, community resources and learning from a response

Preparedness strengthens the conditions in which supportive contact can occur. It includes clear roles, accessible information, verified resource routes and opportunities to practise coordination. It is not merely distributing a handbook or conducting a dramatic simulation. A plan is useful when people understand what to do and the necessary arrangements work under realistic conditions.

A resource map should include ordinary supports as well as specialist services. Transport, food access, medication continuity, interpretation, safe accommodation and help replacing documents may be central to recovery. The map needs a process for checking information because availability and eligibility can change. A directory without responsibility for updating it becomes less reliable over time.

In a fictional neighbourhood exercise, the team discovers that the planned information point cannot be reached by some residents with mobility needs. Another group cannot understand the written notices. These findings are not evidence that residents are unprepared or uncooperative. They identify design problems. The exercise is useful because it reveals what must change before a real emergency.

Evaluation should distinguish different outcomes. Attendance measures reach. A knowledge question can assess understanding. A successful practice handover shows that a procedure worked in that exercise. None alone proves that a programme prevented a mental disorder. A careful evaluation asks whether resources were reached, whether information was understood and whether people experienced the response as respectful and accessible.

Community participation also needs genuine choice. Residents can contribute local knowledge without being expected to replace necessary public or professional services. Different groups may have different priorities, and consultation should not be limited to the most visible voices.

The purpose of preparedness is to create a more usable network of support, not to celebrate resilience while overlooking inadequate resources. Learning from a response involves preserving what worked, identifying gaps and assigning responsibility for improvements. It is a continuing process that connects individual dignity with practical arrangements, coordination and accountability across the community.

Glossary

PFA: Humane, supportive and practical help after serious distressing events.

Look–Listen–Link: A way to organise attention to safety, respectful contact and support connections.

Validation: Acknowledgement of experience without endorsing every conclusion.

Grounding: Optional present- oriented support, not a compulsory calming procedure.

Safeguarding: Responsibilities and procedures that protect people from harm.

Handover: Transfer of relevant information and responsibility through an appropriate route.

Closed-loop referral: Checking receipt and the agreed next action.

Secondary traumatic stress: Trauma-related reactions associated with indirect exposure.

Operational review: Examination of response processes and improvements, distinct from compelled emotional recounting.
